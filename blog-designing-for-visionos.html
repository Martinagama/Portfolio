Spatial Design

Designing for visionOS

Notes from my first project thinking in three dimensions instead of two.

Putting on the Apple Vision Pro for the first time and then opening the visionOS design guidelines right after was disorienting in the best way. Years of designing for rectangles hadn't prepared me for a canvas that has no edges, no fixed size, and a viewer who can simply turn their head and look somewhere else.

Depth is a design decision now

On a flat screen, hierarchy is mostly about size, weight and position. In spatial design, depth joins that list as a first-class tool — how far something sits from the viewer changes how urgent, how ambient, or how ignorable it feels. A control that's slightly closer reads as more important before you've even processed what it says. That's a new kind of hierarchy to design with, and it's easy to overuse it before you learn to trust it.

Designing for eyes and hands

There's no cursor. The primary input is where you look, confirmed by a small pinch of your fingers — which means every target needs to be forgiving, every focus state needs to be obvious, and nothing critical can live at the very edge of comfortable eye movement. I spent more time thinking about hit areas and gaze feedback on this project than on any interaction pattern in a 2D app.

You're not designing a screen anymore. You're designing a piece of space.

Prototyping without a screen

Static mockups stopped being enough pretty quickly — a layout that looks balanced in Figma can feel completely wrong once it's sitting in your actual living room. I moved between Figma for early exploration and Reality Composer Pro for anything involving real depth or scale, and made a habit of testing in the headset earlier than felt comfortable, instead of waiting for a "finished enough" version.

Spatial design isn't just 2D design with an extra axis bolted on. It's a genuinely different set of instincts, and I'm still building mine — one project at a time.

Martina Gama — 2026
